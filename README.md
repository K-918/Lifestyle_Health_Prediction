# Lifestyle_Health_Prediction
Machine learning project for lifestyle-based health prediction and personalized recommendations.

This project develops a machine learning system that uses lifestyle and demographic variables to predict health outcomes and generate personalized lifestyle recommendations. The project is being completed as part of a one-month machine learning internship.

Project Goals:

The project investigates how lifestyle factors such as:

Age

BMI

Exercise frequency

Diet quality

Sleep duration

Smoking status

Alcohol consumption

relate to and can be used to predict Health_Score. The project uses a synthetic health dataset containing 1,000 observations. The dataset includes demographic and lifestyle variables along with a calculated Health_Score.

Important Limitations:

Because Health_Score is a calculated value, machine-learning models may learn the formula or relationships used to generate the score rather than discovering clinically valid health relationships. This project is intended as an educational machine-learning system and is not a diagnostic or medical recommendation tool.

Link to dataset: [https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/pratikyuvrajchougule/health-and-lifestyle-data-for-regression?resource=download]

# EDA
The dataset contains 1,000 observations and includes demographic and lifestyle variables such as Age, BMI, Exercise Frequency, Diet Quality, Sleep Hours, Smoking Status, and Alcohol Consumption. The target variable for the project is Health Score. Initial data inspection showed that the dataset contained no missing values or duplicate rows. I identified several data quality concerns. Negative values were found in Alcohol Consumption, and some Diet Quality values exceeded the expected 0–100 range. These observations were identified and addressed during the data-cleaning process. The interquartile range method was also used to identify potential statistical outliers. Potential outliers were found in Age, BMI, Diet Quality, Sleep Hours, Alcohol Consumption, and Health Score. These observations were kept because statistical outliers are not necessarily data errors and may represent legitimate variation in the dataset. Age values below 18 were also identified and considered during the data-quality investigation, however they were left in the dataset. The correlation analysis showed several relationships between lifestyle variables and Health Score. Diet Quality had the strongest positive correlation with Health Score, suggesting that higher diet-quality values are associated with higher health scores in this dataset. BMI showed a moderate negative correlation with Health Score. Sleep Hours and Exercise Frequency had weaker positive correlations with Health Score. I explored these relationships further using scatter plots, box plots, and a correlation heatmap. I also investigated potential nonlinear relationships and feature interactions using visualizations including scatter plots, box plots, and pair plots. These analyses will help determine whether models capable of capturing nonlinear relationships, such as Random Forest and Gradient Boosting, may perform better than simpler linear models. Based on the EDA, Diet Quality and BMI appear to be particularly important potential predictors of Health Score. Sleep, exercise, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and age may also contribute to predictions. An important limitation is that Health Score is a calculated value rather than an independently measured clinical outcome. Therefore, the machine-learning models may learn relationships or formulas used to generate the synthetic Health Score rather than discovering clinically meaningful health relationships. The results should therefore be interpreted solely as predictions.

# Personalized Health Score Prediction
I focused on predicting an individual's Health Score using lifestyle and demographic information. The goal was to see whether variables such as age, BMI, exercise frequency, diet quality, sleep, smoking status, and alcohol consumption could be used to accurately predict a person's Health Score. After cleaning and exploring the data, I trained several different regression models to predict Health Score. I tested Linear Regression, Ridge Regression, Lasso Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and a Neural Network. I evaluated the models using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and R². The best model should have a low MAE, a low RMSE, and a high R². The initial results showed that the tree-based models generally performed better than the simpler linear models. For example, Linear Regression had an R² of 0.798, while Random Forest achieved 0.829, Gradient Boosting achieved 0.845, and XGBoost achieved 0.851. The Decision Tree performed considerably worse, with an R² of only 0.585. This suggested that the relationships between lifestyle variables and Health Score were probably not completely linear and that the models benefited from being able to capture more complex relationships. I then used five-fold cross-validation to make sure that the results were not only based on one particular train-test split. Interestingly, the cross-validation results changed the ranking of some of the models. The Neural Network performed the best during the initial cross-validation, with an average RMSE of 5.477 and an R² of 0.836. Gradient Boosting followed with an RMSE of 5.667 and an R² of 0.826, while XGBoost had an RMSE of 5.704 and an R² of 0.823. Before cross-validation, XGBoost performed the best. This showed me why it is important not to choose a model based only on one test set. A model can perform extremely well on one particular split but perform differently when shown multiple splits of the data. To improve the strongest 3 models, I used GridSearchCV to tune Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and the Neural Network. The tuned Gradient Boosting model used 300 estimators, a learning rate of 0.1, a maximum depth of 3, and a minimum of 2 samples per leaf. On the test set, this model achieved an MAE of 3.952, an RMSE of 5.254, and an R² of 0.858. The tuned XGBoost model achieved an MAE of 4.014, an RMSE of 5.284, and an R² of 0.857. The tuned Neural Network achieved the lowest test MAE at 3.903, but its RMSE and R² were slightly worse than Gradient Boosting, with an RMSE of 5.405 and an R² of 0.850. Because there was no clear best model after all of this, I selected the tuned Gradient Boosting model as the primary model for the project as it is easiest to work with. Its R² of 0.858 means that it explained approximately 85.8% of the variation in Health Score on the test set. After selecting the model, I wanted to understand which variables were most important to its predictions. The model's feature importance results showed that Diet Quality was by far the most important feature, with an importance of about 0.506. BMI was the second most important at about 0.215. Sleep Hours and Exercise Frequency followed at approximately 0.095 and 0.087, respectively. Age, Alcohol Consumption, and Smoking Status had lower individual importance. I also calculated permutation importance to see how much model performance decreased when each feature was shuffled. The results showed a very similar pattern, with Diet Quality having the largest effect, followed by BMI. This consistency between the two methods made me more confident that the model was relying heavily on these two variables when making predictions. One of the biggest things I learned from this part of the project was that comparing multiple models is more useful than relying on a single algorithm, and that cross-validation and feature analysis are important for understanding whether a model is actually reliable.
